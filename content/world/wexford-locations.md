# Wexford Locations

## Roads and Approaches

<details id="bridgeport-road">
<summary><strong>Bridgeport Road</strong> — the trade road from Bridgeport</summary>

<p>A hard-packed trade road wide enough for two wagons abreast, this is Wexford's main overland artery. From Bridgeport it runs south through open farmland for nearly forty miles, then enters the deciduous canopy of the Wexwood Forest and continues another twelve miles to the town gates. Milestones and drainage ditches mark the route, and the ruts of heavy carts are cut deep where traffic is thickest.</p>

<p>Crown troops patrol the road regularly, and by regional standards it is considered safe. The danger lies in the forest stretch, usually small bands of opportunistic brigands rather than anything organized. Wild beasts mostly keep away by day, though a monster does turn up now and then. There are no real rest stops once the road is under the trees, so most travelers time their journey to arrive well before nightfall, when torchlight carries farther than is comfortable and every sound in the branches feels closer.</p>

</details>

<details id="hibble">
<summary><strong>Hibble</strong> — the riverside hamlet north of the walls</summary>

<p>Hibble sits on a gentle bend of the Wex River roughly five hundred feet north of Wexford's walls. It began as a single seasonal hut used for fishing and swimming and grew into a close cluster of low wooden homes, docks, and reed-thatched shelters built by riverheart mustelins.</p>

<p>Though it lies outside the walls, Hibble is regarded as an informal extension of the town. Its people depend on Wexford for trade, worship, and governance, and in return supply freshwater fish, knowledge of the river, and ferrying. The hamlet is known for its hospitality, its brightly painted river markings, and its communal evenings along the water. Town militia patrols look in regularly, but Hibble's best protection has always been the goodwill it enjoys among Wexford's people.</p>

</details>

<details id="hot-springs-road">
<summary><strong>Hot Springs Road</strong> — the road west to the springs and the coast</summary>

<p>This hard-packed road runs west from Springs Gate and narrows to a single wagon width as it winds through the Wexwood, widening again once it reaches the coastal plain. Crown troops patrol it regularly and it is considered safe, though travelers are advised to keep to daylight hours while under the trees.</p>

<p>Several miles from Wexford the road branches briefly toward the hot springs, a series of naturally heated pools famed for their restorative properties. Farther west it reaches the coast and joins the coastal route north and south, including the way to the fishing town of Cairnellah, where much of Wexford's fresh seafood comes from by cart. The road serves leisure, supply, and travel in equal measure, a quieter but vital artery for the town.</p>

</details>

<details id="westlink-road">
<summary><strong>Westlink Road</strong> — the bypass known as Warden's Cut</summary>

<p>Westlink Road is a hard-packed bypass along the western edge of Wexford that joins Bridgeport Road directly to Springs Road without passing through the town gates. It was first cut as a patrol route by the Wardens and is still commonly called Warden's Cut.</p>

<p>Just outside Springs Gate, where it meets Springs Road, stands a Warden inspection and toll station with a stable. The Wardens there watch the traffic, collect modest tolls when they apply, and point travelers toward the springs or onward routes. The station is built to regulate flow rather than restrict it. Commercial wagons sometimes use the road to avoid congestion, but it is more often taken by visitors coming from Bridgeport who want the springs and have no need of the town. By day it is steadily busy. After dusk traffic thins and patrols increase.</p>

</details>

<details id="riffel-road">
<summary><strong>Riffel Road</strong> — the long road east through the Wexwood</summary>

<p>Riffel Road is the longest and most dangerous stretch of travel through the Wexwood, running east from Wexford through dense woodland to join the Queen's Road near Riffel Station, a Crown garrison at the forest's edge. More than any other route, it serves people who must go into the deep woods rather than skirt them.</p>

<p>Woodcutting camps, scattered ranger stations, and the occasional druidic refuge lie along it, though such places are rarely marked and seldom easy to find. Trade does use the road, but it is limited and purposeful, usually tied to specific contracts or rare materials. The regular trade among Bridgeport, Two Rivers, Rhundel, and Knightsbridge runs along the Queen's Road, with Wexford a western spur and not a central artery.</p>

<p>Crown patrols are responsible for the forest stretch, and the Riffel Station garrison takes over where the road leaves the trees. Even so, travelers are advised to move by daylight and in numbers. Beasts, bandits, and worse have been known to emerge from the deeper forest, especially where the road narrows or bends out of sight. For anyone coming to Wexford from the capital it is the most direct route and often the only practical one, watched but far from tame.</p>

</details>

<details id="river-road">
<summary><strong>River Road</strong> — the lonely road south along the Wex</summary>

<p>The River Road follows the western bank of the Wex River out of Wexford, winding south through the forest before reaching the coastal plain. From there it follows the river as it bends southwest toward the ruins of Nereth, a long-abandoned site where the Wex eventually joins the Gold River. Before the ruins it turns west toward Sand Manor, the coastal seat of the Barony of the Sands.</p>

<p>It is less traveled than the Queen's Road or Riffel Road, used by patrols, messengers, and people with business along the river or at the baronial estate. The route is quieter and more isolated than Wexford's other approaches, and though it is watched at intervals, it runs uncomfortably close to the Dry Fells, the barren hills across the river's eastern bank, where gnoll packs are known to roam the fringes. Travelers take it by choice rather than convenience, and with caution, especially beyond the edge of the forest.</p>

</details>


## Gates and Bridges

<details id="bridgeport-gate">
<summary><strong>Bridgeport Gate</strong> — the north gate and main entrance</summary>

<p>The Bridgeport Gate is Wexford's primary point of entry and exit, handling most of the trade, travel, and official traffic on the north road. Its broad, reinforced gates stand open from dawn until dusk, admitting wagons, caravans, and a steady stream of people on foot.</p>

<p>The Wexford Wardens, the town's civic militia, man the gate. They are easy to spot in their chartreuse tunics or jerkins, and they handle inspections, collect tolls when required, and keep order in the busy hours. Their manner is firm but even-handed, favoring visibility and regulation over force. After dusk the gates are secured, though late arrivals may be admitted at the Wardens' discretion or under Crown authority.</p>

</details>

<details id="springs-gate">
<summary><strong>Springs Gate</strong> — the western gate on the forest road</summary>

<p>Springs Gate is Wexford's second busiest entrance, opening west along the forest road. Like Bridgeport Gate, its heavy wooden doors stand open from dawn until dusk and are manned by Wardens in their chartreuse tunics. The traffic here is steady but less commercial: townsfolk, travelers heading for the hot springs, merchants bringing fish inland, and Crown patrols moving along the western routes.</p>

<p>Because it sits near both recreational sites and outbound forest roads, the Wardens here are used to balancing relaxed civilian traffic with quiet vigilance. Inspections are routine but unintrusive, and the gate is an important outlet for movement that does not pass through the more heavily regulated Bridgeport Gate. The Wardens watch for subtle tells and unfamiliar caravans and step in only when something feels out of place.</p>

</details>

<details id="wexford-bridge">
<summary><strong>Wexford Bridge</strong> — the timber bridge over the Wex</summary>

<p>The Wexford Bridge is a broad timber crossing over the Wex River that replaced the shallow ford which once gave the town its name. Its construction was a turning point in Wexford's growth, turning a seasonal crossing into a permanent route and anchoring the town on the east-west passage between the Queen's Road and the western coast.</p>

<p>It stands on heavy pilings with reinforced decking, built for wagons and foot traffic alike, and its height reflects the shallow river rather than any need to let boats pass beneath. Merchants, travelers, and Crown patrols cross it daily, and it keeps Wexford a dependable link between the interior forests and the wider roads and markets of Algar.</p>

</details>

<details id="eastgate">
<summary><strong>The Eastgate</strong> — the gate into the deep Wexwood</summary>

<p>The Eastgate guards Wexford's most dangerous approach, opening onto the deeper Wexwood where logging camps, rare hardwood stands, and untamed wilds begin. Unlike the town's other gates, it serves work crews, Wardens, and sanctioned expeditions more often than merchants or casual travelers.</p>

<p>To keep traffic controlled and risk low, only one leaf is usually open during the day, letting wagons and people on foot pass in a measured flow. Both leaves open together only for organized convoys, official movements, or festivals when traffic must move quickly and under escort. After dusk the gate is sealed completely, and passing through needs special approval. Experienced Wardens man it, with a cautious respect for what lies beyond. Those who go through are expected to be prepared and provisioned, and to know the forest east of Wexford offers no guarantee of a safe return.</p>

</details>

<details id="spring-creek-bridge">
<summary><strong>Spring Creek Bridge</strong> — the old bridge over the ravine</summary>

<p>The Spring Creek Bridge is a short but solid timber crossing that is many years older than the larger Wexford Bridge. The creek is narrow, but centuries of erosion have cut a deep ravine across the southern edge of town, making a permanent bridge necessary long before the river crossing was formalized.</p>

<p>It was built for strength rather than speed. It is too narrow for wagons to pass each other, so carts cross single file, and the heavy beams and reinforced decking carry loaded traffic easily. Patience is a necessity on market days or during official movements. When the south gate is open, Wardens stand at both ends to manage traffic and quietly note who enters the more affluent southern quarter.</p>

<p>For all its strategic position, the bridge sees little heavy use, and a posting here is slow and often tedious. It remains the controlled link between Wexford's civic core and the estates, barracks, and homes beyond the creek.</p>

</details>

<details id="south-gate">
<summary><strong>South Gate</strong> — the quiet gate to the southern quarter</summary>

<p>The South Gate is the least used of Wexford's four road gates, serving mostly local traffic rather than trade or through travel. On most days only a person-sized door in one of the gate leaves is kept open, and the main gates stay closed unless a wagon, cart, or mounted rider needs through, or a patrol leaves or returns.</p>

<p>The Wardens who man it keep a quiet, deliberate routine. Traffic is light, and those who pass are usually known to the guards or easily accounted for. With the Crown Barracks and the wealthy southern residences close by, the gate is watched carefully, if without the bustle of Bridgeport Gate or Springs Gate. It is unassuming, but it keeps order over the town's southern approaches and makes sure entry into the most secure quarter is neither hurried nor unchecked.</p>

</details>


## Wardens and Crown

<details id="wardenhall">
<summary><strong>Wardenhall</strong> — headquarters of the Wexford Wardens</summary>

<p>Wardenhall is the headquarters of the Wexford Wardens, serving as barracks, administrative center, and training ground. It stands a block west of Bridgeport Gate along the inner wall road, close enough to answer gate traffic, wall alarms, and civic disturbances quickly. The Wardens answer to Captain Rethan Vale.</p>

<p>Inside are shared quarters for Wardens on duty, offices for the watch captains, an armory, and a modest mess hall. A fenced training yard behind the building hosts daily drills and weapons practice, and attached stables hold a small number of mounts. Mounted patrols are uncommon and mostly reserved for escorts or emergencies beyond the walls.</p>

<p>The building is practical and well kept rather than imposing, which suits the Wardens' role as civic stewards rather than soldiers. Its doors are active from before dawn until long after dusk, making it one of the most constantly occupied buildings in town.</p>

</details>

<details id="camber-house">
<summary><strong>Camber House</strong> — the Crown's guesthall</summary>

<p>Camber House is a Crown-owned baronial guesthall kept for visiting nobles, royal inspectors, diplomats, and envoys traveling through the Wexwood. Where the barracks in the southern district show the Crown's strength, Camber House shows its diplomatic face, offering discretion, ceremony, and comfort in place of overt military authority.</p>

<p>The main floor holds a refined lounge and a meeting hall used for private audiences and quiet negotiations. An agent of the Royal Adventuring Society works discreetly from these rooms, arranging sanctioned business as needed. The upper floors hold well-appointed suites that stand mostly empty outside festivals, inspections, and official visits, when the house briefly becomes one of the busiest places in town.</p>

<p>Across a narrow plaza stands an adjoining crenellated watch tower, the third tallest structure in Wexford, which serves as an observation post and signal relay. It is lightly staffed in ordinary times, but during an alert or a royal visit Crown troops may reinforce it, adding to the town's defenses without displacing the Wardens.</p>

</details>

<details id="sands-house">
<summary><strong>Sands House</strong> — the baronial residence and Seneschal's seat</summary>

<p>Sands House is the largest private residence in Wexford, standing apart from everything else in scale and refinement. It is about the size of the Gilded Alder and larger than either of the town's common inns, and it serves as the official baronial residence in town, kept by the Barony of the Sands as Crown property. Seneschal Maereth Ilthandor lives here permanently, and the baron stays when visiting. In the rare event of a royal progress, it is where the Monarch would be received.</p>

<p>The house stands on enclosed, carefully kept grounds of more than half an acre, with formal gardens, private stables, and a small dock on the Wex River. It is undeniably opulent by Wexford's standards, yet the town does not resent it, seeing it instead as a mark of Wexford's importance in the barony. The architecture blends elven-influenced lines with human stonework and timber framing, echoing the rest of the town. It is well guarded but not fortress-like, projecting authority through presence rather than intimidation, and to the people of Wexford it stands for stability, continuity, and the Crown's confidence in the town.</p>

</details>

<details id="crown-barracks">
<summary><strong>Crown Barracks</strong> — the Crown garrison</summary>

<p>The Crown Barracks stand across the road from Sands House, between the southern end of Spring Creek Bridge and the South Gate, at the head of the road to Wexford's wealthiest residences on the south bank of the creek. The barracks mark the shift from civic authority to royal presence.</p>

<p>The building is a fortified stone structure, austere and built for defense and readiness rather than comfort. A fenced training yard adjoins the main building, along with a stable for mounted troops, and the rear of the compound backs onto the steep creek ravine, which adds a natural layer of security.</p>

<p>Crown troops garrison it rather than local Wardens, under Captain Aurix Valemor, and it serves as the base for patrols along Bridgeport Road, Riffel Road, and the other approaches to Wexford. Relations with the civic authorities are generally cooperative, but the separate chains of command are clear. The barracks are a reminder that Wexford, though largely self-governing day to day, stays firmly within the Crown's protection and oversight.</p>

</details>


## Civic Heart and Guilds

<details id="wexford-market">
<summary><strong>Wexford Market</strong> — the plaza market beside Spring Creek</summary>

<p>Wexford Market spreads across a broad plaza in the lee of the town's tallest tower, its stalls clustered near the banks of Spring Creek where water, shade, and foot traffic naturally meet. Canvas awnings, timber frames, and carved signposts give it a distinctly local character, and the murmur of trade carries from morning until late afternoon.</p>

<p>The stalls sell river fish, seafood carted in from the coast, loaves and pastries, forest-hunted meat, hides, leatherwork, and everyday tools. Ordinary weapons and armor are sold openly, though heavier gear usually goes through established guild contacts rather than casual booths. Seasonal produce, herbs, and forest remedies appear in abundance.</p>

<p>Woodcraft dominates. Wexford is renowned for finely worked timber, and the stalls show everything from sturdy furniture to carved chests, musical instruments, charms, toys, and devotional figures. Some pieces use rare woods from the deeper Wexwood, their grain and color unmistakable to a knowledgeable buyer. Guild representatives are a constant presence, watching quality, settling disputes, and arranging bulk orders. Haggling is expected, but outright deception is poorly tolerated, and the market is as much a social center as an economic one, where news travels fast and reputations are quietly made or undone.</p>

</details>

<details id="waywarden-beacon">
<summary><strong>Waywarden Beacon</strong> — the signal tower and its three guildhalls</summary>

<p>The Waywarden Beacon is Wexford's tallest and most prominent structure, rising above the forest canopy as a civic landmark, signal tower, and coordination point during routine alerts. In ordinary times its upkeep and staffing are a purely local matter, handled by civic agreement rather than Crown oversight.</p>

<p>Three conjoined guildhalls enclose the base of the tower on three sides, forming a single complex at the center of the town's economic and administrative life. They belong to the Loggers' &amp; Timberfell Cooperative, the Carpenters' &amp; Shipwrights' Guild, and the Musicians' &amp; Instrument Makers' Fellowship. Each hall opens onto a different thoroughfare: one faces the main north-south road, and the other two open onto cross avenues, including a broad path that follows Spring Creek.</p>

<p>The Beacon itself rises from an enclosed courtyard behind the halls and looks west over Wexford Market. From its upper platform, lanterns, banners, and signal flags guide travelers, mark civic occasions, and warn of trouble in the forest. During alerts its use is coordinated with the Wardens, though it remains a civic instrument and not a military one.</p>

</details>

<details id="adventurers-guild-hall">
<summary><strong>Adventurers' Guild Hall</strong> — the local hall of the Adventurers' Guild</summary>

<p>The Adventurers' Guild Hall occupies a prominent corner where the main north-south road meets the east-west thoroughfare. It is smaller than the Bridgeport Guildhouse but still a substantial building, serving as a regional hub for sanctioned adventurers working in the Wexwood and the lands around it.</p>

<p>The hall has offices for certification and record keeping, briefing rooms, secure storage, and a dedicated area for testing and training applicants. Its facilities are more limited than those of the larger city branches, but the Wexford hall is fully empowered to issue and recognize Guild certifications, coordinate sanctioned work, and liaise with local authorities.</p>

<p>The Guild keeps a steady relationship with the Wardens, the town council, and the nearby guilds, and often acts as an intermediary when skilled outsiders are needed. For many adventurers it is the last formal checkpoint before the deeper forest or the more dangerous frontiers beyond.</p>

</details>

<details id="moot-of-wexford">
<summary><strong>The Moot of Wexford</strong> — the town's civic gathering hall</summary>

<p>The Moot of Wexford is the town's primary civic gathering hall, standing apart from guild, noble, and religious authority. It was built for large assemblies and hosts seasonal feasts, public addresses, dispute arbitrations, and council sessions that need open attendance or witnesses. When a matter affects the whole town, this is where it is heard. It faces the Shrine of Olya Greenbriar across the plaza.</p>

<p>The hall is timber-framed and spacious, with long hearths, heavy beams, and movable tables that let it shift easily between feast, forum, and tribunal. Individual guilds keep their own halls, but the Moot exists for matters that rise above trade or station, underlining Wexford's identity as a cooperative, self-governing community.</p>

<p>During festivals or times of concern it becomes one of the busiest places in town. The Seneschal or Crown representatives may attend by invitation, but the hall remains firmly under civic control, a symbol of the balance Wexford keeps between royal oversight and local autonomy.</p>

</details>

<details id="feyward-spire">
<summary><strong>Feyward Spire</strong> — the civic tower and fey crossing plaza</summary>

<p>The Feyward Spire is the second-highest tower in Wexford, standing on top of and partly built into the civic administration building at the town's heart. Its stonework shows the restrained elegance seen across Wexford, blending human practicality with elven-influenced lines that draw the eye upward without excess ornament. Wardens and officials once kept watch over the forest approaches from its upper levels. Today it is a symbolic and working anchor for the town's government.</p>

<p>A broad civic plaza lies before the main entrance, paved with a carefully inlaid replica of a circular fey crossing. The pattern is inert and purely commemorative, marking the place where Wexford's founders believe the veil between worlds once thinned. It is kept up exactly as it is, neither embellished nor erased, a quiet acknowledgment of the town's origins rather than a place of active reverence.</p>

<p>Opposite the crossing stands a massive statue of an ancient aelfaen watcher in heroic pose, vigilant and composed, neither threatening nor welcoming. For many half-elves it speaks of ancestral duty and watchfulness. For others it is a reminder that Wexford was once a threshold between realms, shaped as much by restraint as by ambition. The spire and plaza sit near the meeting of Spring Creek and the Wex River, a natural and symbolic crossroads where civic announcements, assemblies, and ceremonies are held.</p>

</details>


## Inns

<details id="limping-fiddler">
<summary><strong>The Limping Fiddler Inn</strong> — the adventurer-friendly inn</summary>

<p>The Limping Fiddler is Wexford's most accessible and adventurer-friendly lodging, run by Elowen Thistledown. It stands just off the main north-south road on a diagonal side street, a short walk from the civic plaza and directly across from the open square that holds the fey crossing memorial, the great statue, and the town administration building. That makes it a natural gathering place for newcomers.</p>

<p>The busy ground-floor tavern is known for loud evenings, shared tables, and live music more often than not. Two floors of guest rooms above offer modest but clean accommodation favored by travelers, scouts, and sanctioned adventurers. Behind the inn lies a fenced stable yard backing onto the main road, so guests can arrive and leave without crossing the plaza. A detached blacksmith's workshop shares the yard, and its forge serves the inn's guests and passing traffic.</p>

<p>It is far from refined, but dependable, well placed, and used to trouble, a preferred stop for those who plan a short stay or arrive with stories best told over ale.</p>

</details>

<details id="gilded-alder">
<summary><strong>The Gilded Alder Inn</strong> — the refined inn</summary>

<p>The Gilded Alder is Wexford's most refined inn, offering comfort and discretion without the airs of a noble house. Its ground floor is dressed stone, supporting two upper levels of finely kept timberwork that show off the town's craftsmanship. Inside are a well-appointed lounge, a formal dining room, and a common room that favors quiet conversation and attentive service.</p>

<p>All are welcome, but it naturally draws a more discerning clientele. Townsfolk choose it for celebrations and rare indulgences, and its private rooms suit merchants of means, diplomats, guild envoys, and travelers used to good lodging. The kitchen leans on local ingredients, wood-fired cooking, and seasonal dishes.</p>

<p>Behind the inn, a carriage house at the end of a tree-lined lane offers sheltered parking and stabling away from the street, so guests can come and go with little notice, a feature prized by anyone conducting sensitive business. Polished but never exclusive, the Alder reflects Wexford's quieter prosperity and respect for craft.</p>

</details>

<details id="laughing-bole">
<summary><strong>The Laughing Bole Tavern</strong> — the tavern by the bridge</summary>

<p>The Laughing Bole is a modest inn and public house on the main east road where it enters the Wexwood, serving travelers from Riffel Station and the Queen's Road as well as laborers from the mills and workshops on the east side. It stands beside Wexford Bridge, an easy first or last stop in town, with the civic plaza a short walk across the river. Caelen Mossbarrel tends the bar.</p>

<p>The tavern offers plain lodging, filling meals, and a well-used common room where workers, traders, and passing travelers mix freely. Prices are close to those at the Limping Fiddler, and the mood is just as unpretentious, favoring warmth, noise, and familiarity over refinement. A fenced stable next door is often busy on market days or when forest crews come back to town. Millhands fill it at the end of long shifts, and so do travelers reluctant to cross the bridge late at night. It is a dependable place to rest, drink, and catch the latest talk from the road.</p>

</details>


## Temples and Healing

<details id="temple-of-the-lantern-keeper">
<summary><strong>Temple of the Lantern-Keeper</strong> — temple of Mollo, the Lantern-Keeper</summary>

<p>This welcoming temple of stone and timber is dedicated to <a href="{{< relref "the-gods#mollo" >}}">Mollo</a>, Ascendant of Hope, Warmth, and Night Vigil, known across the region as the Lantern-Keeper. Its windows glow softly after dusk, and a tall beacon lantern burns each night from a towered roof, visible above the trees and from the forest roads approaching the town from the north.</p>

<p>The temple's prominence reflects Wexford's place in the Wexwood, a forest some sixty-five miles wide from east to west and thirty from north to south. By day it is largely peaceful, but its southern reaches lie uncomfortably close to the Dry Fells, and on rare occasions gnolls and other hostile creatures slip out of that wasteland under cover of night to raid isolated huts, hunting lodges, and logging camps.</p>

<p>The temple answers as a spiritual bulwark, not a fortress. Devotees keep nightly vigils, tend lantern beacons along nearby forest paths, and offer shelter, healing, and warmth to anyone arriving after dark. Rangers, woodcutters, and patrols often stop for a brief devotion before heading into the deeper woods, seeking resolve rather than protection alone. Within town the temple works closely with the Wardens, supplying watch rotations during alerts and organizing aid when trouble stirs beyond the walls.</p>

</details>

<details id="marble-canon">
<summary><strong>The Marble Canon</strong> — temple of Absot, the largest building in town</summary>

<p>The Marble Canon is the largest and most imposing structure in Wexford, filling an entire city block near Springs Gate. It was built from pale marble quarried in the North Wall, a mountain range in northern Algar, and carried to Wexford at great expense by land and sea under the direction of dwarven masons. The result is a monolithic, carefully proportioned temple ringed by trees and a landscaped garden, its clean lines and measured symmetry deliberately free of ornament. Hierophant Halwen Marr presides.</p>

<p>The temple is dedicated to <a href="{{< relref "the-gods#absot" >}}">Absot</a>, Eternal of cause and effect, destiny's mechanisms, and cosmic order. Absot is neither merciful nor cruel, only inevitable. Devotees do not come seeking absolution. They come for understanding, resolve, and the discipline to change future outcomes through deliberate action, or to accept the paths that can no longer be changed and take comfort in an ordered universe.</p>

<p>Inside, the space is quiet and restrained. Marble floors are etched with geometric patterns and linear motifs suggesting progression and consequence, and the clergy offer measured counsel rather than reassurance, helping petitioners see where effort, restraint, or perseverance might still alter their fate. In Absot's doctrine redemption is not granted but earned through sustained, meaningful action. Judges, guild representatives, travelers at personal crossroads, and even Crown officials pass through its doors, often without ceremony. The Canon inspires no fervor, but its influence on the town's decisions is deeply felt.</p>

</details>

<details id="shrine-of-olya-greenbriar">
<summary><strong>Shrine of Olya Greenbriar</strong> — the garden shrine on the plaza</summary>

<p>At the northwest corner of the plaza commemorating Wexford's ancient fey crossing stands the Shrine of <a href="{{< relref "the-gods#olya" >}}">Olya Greenbriar</a>, the smallest of the town's temples and among the most visible. It sits near the Limping Fiddler and the Gilded Alder, directly across from the Moot, facing the main east-west thoroughfare where daily life streams by.</p>

<p>The shrine is a low stone building softened by living things. Planters, trellises, and shallow garden beds press against its walls, blurring the line between street and sanctuary. Above the entrance, a stone relief shows Olya's symbol: an open palm holding a three-leaf plant that rises among stylized buildings, repeated in smaller motifs throughout the grounds. It stands for care rather than command, growth sustained through stewardship, and nature thriving inside the spaces people build.</p>

<p>Devotion here is practical and unceremonious. Gardeners, artisans, builders, and townsfolk tending courtyards or allotments stop to leave offerings of water, seeds, cuttings, or soil. Worshippers ask Olya less for abundance than for endurance: the patience to rebuild, the resolve to maintain, and the strength to nurture life in cramped places. A small circle of caretakers tends the shrine, thinking of themselves less as clergy than as keepers of balance between growth and settlement. Its Prior, the Aelfaen druid Thalanor Briarstep, speaks for the town's gardeners and caretakers and holds a seat on the Council of Six.</p>

</details>

<details id="copper-hospice">
<summary><strong>The Copper Hospice</strong> — the town's house of healing</summary>

<p>Tucked into the northern corner of east Wexford, where the town wall meets the Wex River, the Copper Hospice is regarded as one of the town's most vital institutions. To the people of Wexford it is a place of succor and skilled care, where those too sick, injured, or wounded to recover at home receive constant attention from trained healers and attendants. Matron Ilyra Fencrest runs it.</p>

<p>The hospice is the largest building in a small enclosed compound, and its doors are open day and night. Few in town question the quiet efficiency with which patients are treated or the steady presence of staff who seem well used to hardship. Serious injuries, lingering illnesses, and the aftermath of forest accidents are common sights, and many lives have been saved here.</p>

<p>Two smaller buildings stand beside it. One holds kitchens, common rooms, and modest quarters for staff and long-term patients, and the other handles administration, record keeping, and communal meals. Their routine activity draws little notice.</p>

</details>


## Mills and Timber

<details id="greenbriar-timber-works">
<summary><strong>Greenbriar Timber Works</strong> — the waterwheel sawmill on the west bank</summary>

<p>This sprawling timber concern fills a broad stretch of riverbank where the Wex runs strong enough to turn a large waterwheel. The wheel drives a heavy reciprocating saw frame, and its steady clatter carries across the yards and sheds. The river is too shallow to carry cargo, but it supplies reliable power for most of the year.</p>

<p>Logs arrive by cart and wagon from forest camps and logging roads and are hauled into place with winches and sloped runs. Finished boards are stacked in open lumber yards, drying racks, barns, and covered work sheds, sorted by grade and size before going to local workshops or out of town by road. The Loggers' &amp; Timberfell Cooperative oversees the works, with regular inspections from the Carpenters' &amp; Shipwrights' Guild to keep quality consistent. It is one of the town's primary economic engines, turning forest labor and running water into the materials that underpin much of Wexford's prosperity.</p>

</details>

<details id="ironroot-lumber">
<summary><strong>Ironroot Lumber &amp; Milling</strong> — the east-bank sawmill</summary>

<p>Ironroot Lumber &amp; Milling holds the southern tip of east Wexford along the Wex and is the town's second major timber concern and its most openly industrial. Like Greenbriar across the river, it uses a river-driven waterwheel to power heavy saw frames and cutting gear, but its layout favors volume and efficiency over refinement.</p>

<p>A broad lumber yard stacked with raw logs and cut boards surrounds the mill, and sheds and warehouses crowd the grounds, holding drying racks, tool stores, and half-finished materials. Across the road, independent carpentry shops work closely with the mill, drawing stock straight from its piles to make beams, crates, wagons, and other plain goods. Logs arrive only by wagon and cart from the forest camps, and finished lumber leaves the same way for building sites, guild workshops, and overland trade.</p>

<p>The mill keeps strong ties to the Loggers' &amp; Timberfell Cooperative, though its workforce has more hired laborers and seasonal crews, which gives the yard a busier, rougher feel. Visitors see it less than the civic and religious centers, but it matters a great deal to the town's economy, and the creak of timbers and smell of fresh-cut wood mark this edge of Wexford as a place of work first and ceremony last.</p>

</details>
